While researching at NBDA Asia in Singapore, I saw a challenge that many businesses still face. A great deal has been written about what helps innovation succeed, and research has identified many of the same factors across industries and over time.

Yet many organizations still struggle to turn what they know into action.

Small and medium-sized businesses can find it difficult to apply research and ideas to everyday decisions. They may know they should innovate, but not know how to move from their current way of working to a stronger, more consistent approach. My research focused on creating a tool to help them assess their starting point and plan improvements.

Three parts of successful innovation

Innovation is not just a flash of inspiration. It depends on three things working together: the organization, its people and the market. A promising idea can struggle if the organization cannot support it, people lack the skills or time, or customers do not need the result.

The Alignment Framework

I. All aligned: The organization, its people and the market are ready.

II. Skills gap: The organization and market are ready, but people need more support or skills.

III. Customer need gap: The organization and its people are ready, but customers do not need the idea.

IV. Delivery gap: People and customers are ready, but the organization is not set up to deliver.

Four areas to strengthen

The research identified four areas that can help organizations move from reacting to daily problems to supporting innovation more consistently. These findings draw on design research and interviews with leaders of small and medium-sized businesses in Singapore.

01. Strategy

Start with customer needs

A strong strategy starts with real customer needs. In my interviews, many businesses made decisions mainly around their own operations and existing products.

  • Instead of: Selling what the company already makes.
  • Consider: Creating products and services customers need.
  • Aim: Question assumptions and find better ways to serve customers.
02. Understand the market

Find needs customers may not state

Do more than ask customers whether they like a product. Observe how they work and look for problems they may not be able to describe.

  • Instead of: Relying only on satisfaction surveys.
  • Consider: Working with customers to create useful solutions.
  • Aim: Learn from people who are already facing emerging needs.
03. Use a clear process

Test ideas and learn

Innovation does not depend on a single flash of inspiration. Give teams a clear way to explore ideas, test them and decide what to do next.

  • Instead of: Expecting every idea to work immediately.
  • Consider: Testing ideas early and learning from the results.
  • Aim: Make room for experiments while keeping day-to-day work on track.
04. Support new ideas

Make it safe to speak up

Leaders can support innovation by creating an environment where people can share ideas, ask questions and challenge familiar ways of working.

  • Instead of: Punishing every unsuccessful idea.
  • Consider: Treating well-run tests as a chance to learn.
  • Aim: Encourage people to question assumptions and share what they discover.

Assessing progress over time

The research produced a tool that assesses an organization’s innovation practices on an eight-level scale. The scale is intended to show how consistently innovation is supported, from a mostly reactive approach to one where learning and new ideas are part of everyday work.

At the early stages, teams focus mainly on daily problems. At more advanced stages, customer feedback informs decisions, people have time and resources to test ideas, and the organization is willing to challenge its own products and assumptions.

Maturity Level Characteristic The Reality
Level 1: Vague Mostly reacting to problems "We focus on daily operations. We talk about innovation, but have little time to plan for the future."
Level 3: Described A plan, but limited follow-through "We have an innovation plan and set aside some money, but it is not closely connected to daily work. We stop projects if they do not show results quickly."
Level 5: Bottom-Up Staff and customer input "Customer feedback helps shape our plans. We set aside time and money for a range of projects, including longer-term ideas."
Level 8: Machine Regularly renewing the business "We question our assumptions and are willing to replace our own products when better solutions emerge."

Lessons from the Singapore research

The interviews highlighted how a strong focus on finding the one correct answer can make businesses cautious about risk.

Fear of failure can stop employees from sharing ideas. When every unsuccessful idea is punished, people may stop suggesting new ones. Organizations can instead treat carefully planned tests as a way to learn.

Some of the more innovative businesses created protected spaces to test ideas without disrupting their day-to-day operations.

What organizations can do

A score is only useful if it helps an organization decide what to do next. An assessment should show the difference between current practices and the desired state, then help create a practical improvement plan.

  • 1

    Check the problem first: Early on, find out whether the problem is real before trying to prove that a solution will succeed.

  • 2

    Set clear boundaries: Giving a team a focused problem to solve can be more useful than asking it to explore without limits.

  • 3

    Balance learning and delivery: Explore enough to find promising ideas, then focus on developing and delivering the strongest ones.
