Why Some Businesses Should Be Built Around AI from the Start

What past technology shifts can teach us about building useful AI-powered businesses.

Jille Kuipers
Published on 3 February 2026, 12:00 • Hong Kong
Save (PDF)

I was reading Y Combinator’s latest Request for Startups and one theme stood out: building businesses around AI from the start. Many companies add new technology to products and processes that already exist. That can be useful, but it may not be enough. In some cases, the bigger opportunity is to rethink how the business works and build a new service around what the technology can do.

Why established companies can struggle

Years ago, I spoke with the CEO of a large insurance company about the future. Changing direction felt like turning a tanker: the company had years of data, established processes and a culture built around doing things a familiar way. Moving slowly felt safer, but it also made change difficult.

Research by Abernathy and Utterback describes how industries often move from a period of experimentation to a more settled phase. As products and processes become established, companies can become more efficient—but also less flexible.

New competitors may start with small, simple products that address needs larger companies have overlooked. If customers find them useful, these products can grow and change what the industry expects. Over time, a new generation of products and services can take the place of the old.

S Curves & The AI Shift

At university, I studied examples of major technology shifts: film cameras to digital cameras, feature phones to smartphones, and successive changes in the lighting industry. I have seen established companies invest heavily in new technology. Whether they remain successful often depends on whether they are also willing to change their business models and ways of working.

"We may be at the beginning of a new wave of change."

Customer expectations keep changing

Customers carry their experiences from one industry to another. A fast, helpful service in one area can raise expectations for healthcare, insurance and retail. Companies are therefore compared not only with direct competitors, but also with the best experiences customers have elsewhere. When customer needs are changing quickly, rigid processes can make it harder for established businesses to keep up.

AI can also support work in the physical world

Another idea in Y Combinator’s request was using AI to guide physical work. It reminded me of a project I saw years ago that explored augmented reality for medical procedures.

At the time, the technology was not ready: virtual reality could disorient surgeons, while augmented-reality headsets had short battery life and delays. Newer AI systems can interpret images and spoken instructions, which could make it possible to guide someone through a task in real time.

Research by Abernathy and Utterback suggests that, when an industry is changing quickly, new ideas are often driven by customer needs and how well a product works—not only by cost. This could matter in areas such as:

  • Metal production: Using software to improve speed and flexibility, rather than focusing only on volume.
  • AI-focused agencies: Delivering more work through software instead of relying only on billable hours.
  • Healthcare: Helping professionals carry out physical tasks, rather than replacing their expertise.

Why simply adding AI may not be enough

Organizations face a trade-off: the processes that make an established business efficient can make it harder to test very different products and services.

This is why building a business around AI from the start may matter. For example, a financial firm may get limited value from a new AI tool if its existing approval processes and systems cannot support a different way of working. Some companies may need to build new services and processes around the technology, rather than trying to fit the technology into every existing process.

Older products and services can remain profitable. At the same time, new approaches are emerging. Companies need to decide how to support today’s business while building what customers may need next.
